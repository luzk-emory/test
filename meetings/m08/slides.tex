\section{The Estimand: The Proposal}
% ====================================================================

% [RELEASE R1 — narrative and six-row table]
\begin{frame}{Meridian Hotels}

\textbf{Wen} is commercial director of a group of 80 properties.

\bigskip

A revenue management (RM) system sets the rate for every hotel-night from forecast demand, competitor rates, booking pace and the event calendar. \textbf{No human sets these rates night by night}: the system is an automated decision-maker --- and, as in M7, its decisions are what the analysis must adjust for.

\bigskip
\pause

\textbf{On her desk:} sales proposes raising midweek premium rates by \textbf{8\%} across the portfolio.

\bigskip

Their evidence is the RM system's own reporting: nights with higher rates sell more rooms.

\bigskip

Current rate \textyen 800. Variable cost \textyen 200 per occupied room. Three years of logs: 80 hotels $\times$ 1{,}095 nights, 47 recorded signals per night.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Number That Decides It}

An 8\% rise takes contribution per occupied room from \textyen 600 to \textyen 664, a gain of \textbf{10.7\%}.

\bigskip

The rise pays only if bookings fall by \emph{less} than that.

\bigskip
\pause

\begin{alertblock}{Break-even elasticity}
\[
\eta^{*} = \frac{\ln(600/664)}{\ln(1.08)} = \mathbf{-1.32}
\]
\end{alertblock}

\bigskip

Every estimate today is an elasticity. The only question is \textbf{which side of $-1.32$} it lands on.
\end{frame}

Same six nights. Opposite sign.

\]

\bigskip

\end{frame}

\section{Identification and Estimation: Residualisation}
% ====================================================================

% [RELEASE R2 — residual columns]
\begin{frame}{Subtract What the System Already Knew}

Write demand as
\[
Q = \theta P + g(X) + \varepsilon, \qquad \E[\varepsilon \mid P, X] = 0 .
\]

\bigskip
\pause

Define the two \textbf{conditional expectations}
\[
\ell(X) = \E[Q \mid X], \qquad r(X) = \E[P \mid X].
\]

\bigskip

Subtracting them removes everything $X$ can explain from \emph{both} sides:

\begin{alertblock}{Frisch--Waugh--Lovell}
\[
Q - \ell(X) \;=\; \theta \,\bigl\{P - r(X)\bigr\} \;+\; \varepsilon
\]
\end{alertblock}

\bigskip

The coefficient from the full regression \textbf{equals} the slope of one residual on the other.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Two Nuisances, One Parameter}

\[
\underbrace{Q - \ell(X)}_{\widetilde Q}
\;=\; \theta \underbrace{\bigl\{P - r(X)\bigr\}}_{\widetilde P} \;+\; \varepsilon
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\widehat\theta = \frac{\sum_i \widetilde P_i \widetilde Q_i}{\sum_i \widetilde P_i^{\,2}}
\]

\bigskip
\pause

\begin{block}{A warning about $\ell$ and $g$}
Predicting $Q$ from $X$ estimates $\ell(X) = \theta\, r(X) + g(X)$, \textbf{not} $g(X)$.

\medskip

$\ell$ already contains the part of price that $X$ explains. That is exactly why subtracting $\ell$ from $Q$ and $r$ from $P$ leaves a clean comparison.
\end{block}

\bigskip
\pause

$\theta$ is the one thing we want. $\ell$ and $r$ are \textbf{nuisances}: needed, but not of interest.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
% [MERGED from old-decks/lecture08.tex:547, re-skinned to Meridian]
\begin{frame}{What the Residuals Mean}

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[

]
\node[block, fill=noteblue!10] (X)     at (5, 2.1)  {demand signals $X$\\(what the system saw)};
\node[block, fill=gray!10]     (Ptil)  at (0, 0)    {$\widetilde P = P - r(X)$\\``surprise rate''};
\node[block, fill=gray!10]     (Qtil)  at (10, 0)   {$\widetilde Q = Q - \ell(X)$\\``surprise bookings''};
\node[block, fill=sbgreen!15]  (theta) at (5, -1.3) {$\widehat{\theta} = $ slope of $\widetilde Q$ on $\widetilde P$};
\draw[-{Stealth}, noteblue] (X) -- node[left, font=\scriptsize]{predict the rate} (Ptil);
\draw[-{Stealth}, noteblue] (X) -- node[right, font=\scriptsize]{predict bookings} (Qtil);
\draw[-{Stealth}, sbgreen, thick]  (Ptil) -- (theta);
\draw[-{Stealth}, sbgreen, thick]  (Qtil) -- (theta);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\medskip

$\widetilde P$: a night the system priced \emph{above} (or below) what its own signals called for.

$\widetilde Q$: a night that booked \emph{above} (or below) what the same signals predicted.

\medskip

$\widehat{\theta}$ asks one question: on nights the rate came in higher than the signals called for, did bookings come in lower? A night priced exactly by rule has $\widetilde P = 0$ and \textbf{answers nothing}.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{What Must Be True}

\begin{alertblock}{Identifying assumption}
Given the signals the system recorded, the rate it charged is as good as randomly assigned.
\[
\varepsilon \indep P \mid X
\]
\end{alertblock}

\bigskip

Tied to Meridian:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The RM system is the \emph{only} thing setting rates \hfill (no unrecorded human input)
    \item Every input it used is in the feed \hfill (nothing it saw is missing from $X$)
    \item Rates still move for reasons unrelated to demand \hfill (something left to compare)
    \item Every signal is measured \emph{as of} the moment the rate was set \hfill (no post-treatment control)
\end{itemize}

\bigskip
\pause

\textbf{Hold onto the first one.} We come back to it at the end of the hour.

\small Booking pace recorded \emph{after} the rate posted, or a competitor index that reacts to Meridian's own rate, is partly caused by the rate: a mediator or collider (M7), not a control.
\end{frame}

\section{Estimation: Three Attempts}
% ====================================================================

% [R3 is handed out at "Predict Before You Look" — see the marker there]
\begin{frame}{The Real Logs}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
Hotel-nights & 87{,}600 \\
Recorded signals & 47 \\
Median rate & \textyen 803 \quad (5th--95th: \textyen 439--\textyen 1{,}920) \\
Median premium rooms booked & 61 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\bigskip
\pause

The RM system is \textbf{rule-based}. Log rate is a step function of bucketed signals:

\begin{itemize}\small
    \item six forecast-occupancy bands, competitor-index bands, booking-pace bands
    \item an event flag interacted with a lead-time tercile
    \item day-of-week and month adjustments, plus a weekend rule that differs by occupancy band
\end{itemize}

\bigskip

Around that rule, rates still move for reasons tied to no recorded signal.

\bigskip
\textbf{In pairs:} write down the model you would fit. Three proposals go on the board.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{From Slope to Elasticity}

Wen needs a percentage, not yuan per room. Put both sides in logs:

\[
\ln Q = \theta \ln P + g(X) + \varepsilon
\]

\bigskip

Now $\theta$ is the \textbf{elasticity}: the \% change in bookings from a 1\% change in rate.

\bigskip
\pause

\begin{itemize}
    \item The model is \textbf{linear in $\ln P$} --- a local approximation, good near observed rates
    \item It is \textbf{unrestricted in $X$} --- $g$ can be any shape at all
\end{itemize}

\bigskip

\begin{center}
Break-even is $\mathbf{-1.32}$. Keep it on the board.
\end{center}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Attempt 1 --- Put All 47 Controls in Linearly}

\[
\ln Q_i = \theta \ln P_i + X_i'\beta + \varepsilon_i
\]

\bigskip
\pause

\begin{center}
\Large $\widehat\theta = \textcolor{warnred}{-0.54}$ \qquad $\Longrightarrow$ \qquad \textcolor{warnred}{\textbf{raise}}
\end{center}

\bigskip
\pause

\textbf{Why it fails.} The rule is a \emph{staircase}, and the model is a \emph{ramp}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A linear term in occupancy cannot represent six discrete bands
    \item An additive model cannot represent \textsc{weekend} $\times$ \textsc{occupancy band}
    \item Whatever the controls miss stays in the error and keeps confounding $\theta$
\end{itemize}

\bigskip

\begin{center}
\small Getting the functional form wrong is not a small sin when $X$ drives both sides.
\end{center}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Attempt 2 --- Let ML Fit It, With Price as a Feature}

Fit a boosted model of $\ln Q$ on $(\ln P, X)$; read the elasticity off the fitted model.

\bigskip
\pause

\begin{center}
\Large $\widehat\theta = \textcolor{warnred}{-1.03}$ \qquad $\Longrightarrow$ \qquad \textcolor{warnred}{\textbf{raise}}
\end{center}

\bigskip
\pause

\textbf{Why it fails.} Price is a near-deterministic function of $X$, so the model uses it \textbf{as a stand-in} for the signals:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Model of bookings} & \textbf{$R^2$} & \textbf{comp.\ index share} & \textbf{$\ln P$ share} \\
\midrule
on $X$ only & 0.951 & 4.2\% & --- \\
on $X$ and $\ln P$ & 0.970 & \textcolor{warnred}{0.9\%} & 17.6\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\bigskip

The confounder's share falls by \textbf{80\%}. Its effect is loaded onto price.

\bigskip
A good \emph{prediction} of bookings is not a good \emph{estimate} of $\theta$.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Attempt 3 --- Residualise, Fitting the Models on All Rows}

The right idea: predict $\ln Q$ and $\ln P$ from $X$, regress residual on residual.

\bigskip

The wrong execution: fit both models on \emph{all} rows, then predict \emph{those same} rows.

\bigskip
\pause

\begin{center}
\Large $\widehat\theta = \textcolor{warnred}{-0.95}$ \qquad $\Longrightarrow$ \qquad \textcolor{warnred}{\textbf{raise}}
\end{center}

\bigskip
\pause

\textbf{Why it fails.} A flexible model partly \textbf{memorises} each row.

\begin{itemize}
    \item In-sample $R^2 = 0.97$; the honest out-of-sample figure is far lower
    \item Residuals come out too small, and the two residuals' errors are correlated
    \item That correlation lands directly on $\theta$
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{And You Cannot Size the Bias}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
\textbf{How the nuisance models were fitted} & $\widehat\theta$ \\
\midrule
Well-regularised model, on all rows & $-1.57$ \\
Well-regularised model, cross-fitted & $-1.56$ \\
\midrule
Flexible model, on all rows & \textcolor{warnred}{$-0.95$} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\bigskip
\pause

\begin{alertblock}{The real lesson}
The bias grows with how flexible the nuisance model is --- and you have \textbf{no way to know how big it is} from inside the fit.
\end{alertblock}

\bigskip

Cross-fitting removes the need to guess.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\section{Estimation: Cross-Fitting}
% ====================================================================

\begin{frame}{Cross-Fitting}

Split the rows into $K$ folds. Predict each fold from models that \textbf{never saw it}.

\bigskip

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[
    cell/.style={draw, minimum width=1.9cm, minimum height=0.62cm, font=\scriptsize},
    tr/.style={cell, fill=gray!12, draw=gray!55},
    te/.style={cell, fill=sbgreen!25, draw=sbgreen, thick}
]
\foreach \k in {1,...,4} {
  \node[font=\scriptsize, anchor=east] at (-0.15, -0.72*\k) {fold \k\ scored:};
  \foreach \j in {1,...,4} {
     \pgfmathparse{int(\j)}
     \ifnum\j=\k
        \node[te] at (1.95*\j, -0.72*\k) {predict};
     \else
        \node[tr] at (1.95*\j, -0.72*\k) {train};
     \fi
  }
}
\foreach \j in {1,...,4} {
  \node[font=\scriptsize, anchor=south] at (1.95*\j, -0.35) {block \j};
}
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\medskip

Two models per fold: one for $\ln Q$, one for $\ln P$. Stack the out-of-fold residuals, then

\[
\widehat\theta = \frac{\sum_i \widetilde P_i \widetilde Q_i}{\sum_i \widetilde P_i^{\,2}} .
\]

\smallskip
\small Every row is predicted exactly once, by a model trained elsewhere.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Why Residualise \emph{Both} Sides?}

Write the nuisance errors as $e_r = \widehat r - r$ and $e_\ell = \widehat\ell - \ell$.

\bigskip

The bias in $\widehat\theta$ is \textbf{second order}: it depends on products of the errors --- $e_r \cdot e_\ell$, and the price model's error with itself, $e_r^2$ --- not on either error alone.

\bigskip
\pause

\begin{block}{The practical consequence}
Two models that are each \emph{moderately} wrong give a bias that is \emph{very} small.

\medskip

Model only one side and its error enters directly --- that is Attempt 2.
\end{block}

\bigskip
\pause

This is why the method survives ML nuisances at all: boosted trees are biased, and products of small errors are negligible. (The price model matters twice: fit it well.)

\bigskip
\small (The formal statement --- Neyman orthogonality and the rate conditions --- is in the notes.)
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
% [MERGED from old-decks/lecture08.tex:452, re-skinned to Meridian]
\begin{frame}{Why DML Needs Both Ingredients}

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
\textbf{Problem} & \textbf{Where you saw it} & \textbf{Source} & \textbf{Fix} \\
\midrule
Regularisation bias & Attempt 2: \textcolor{warnred}{$-1.03$} & price stands in for & residualise \emph{both} sides \\
 & & the signals it shrinks & (bias is a product of errors) \\[0.5em]
Overfitting bias & Attempt 3: \textcolor{warnred}{$-0.95$} & nuisance models memorise & cross-fit \\
 & & the rows they score & (predict each row out of fold) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\bigskip
\pause

\textbf{Residualising alone is not enough:} Attempt 3 residualised both sides and still recommended the rise.

\medskip

\textbf{Cross-fitting alone is not enough:} a cross-fitted model of bookings on $(\ln P, X)$ still lets price absorb the signals it shrinks.

\medskip

\textbf{Together:} $\widehat\theta = \textcolor{sbgreen}{-1.56}$, with an interval you can use.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Inference}

\textbf{Standard error} comes from the residual regression. Two cautions at Meridian:

\bigskip

\begin{itemize}
    \item The group adjusts rates system-wide on some nights, and demand shocks hit every hotel on the same night. Treating rows as independent makes the SE about \textbf{three times too small}.
    \bigskip
    \item Folds should be assigned \textbf{by night}, not by row, or the shared nightly move leaks across folds.
\end{itemize}

\bigskip
\pause

\begin{center}
Cross-fitting fixes the \emph{estimate}. Neither of these does --- they are about the \emph{interval}.
\end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Do Not Read the Formula Too Literally}

Constant elasticity plus constant marginal cost implies an optimal rate
\[
P^{*} = c\,\frac{\theta}{1+\theta} = 200 \times \frac{-1.56}{-0.56} \approx 557 \text{ yuan}.
\]

\bigskip
\pause

\begin{alertblock}{Should Wen cut rates by 30\%?}
\textbf{No.} Holding the signals fixed, the system varied rates over roughly \textyen 703--\textyen 910. \textyen 557 is far outside anything the data ever saw.
\end{alertblock}

\bigskip

$\theta$ is a \textbf{local slope}, not a global demand curve.

\bigskip

What the evidence supports: \emph{do not raise}, and a modest cut is worth testing --- deliberately, with randomised variation.

\small And the formula inherits the interval: with $\kappa = -\theta$, $P^* = 200\,\kappa/(\kappa - 1)$ maps the CI $\kappa \in [1.51, 1.64]$ to $P^* \in [\text{\textyen}513, \text{\textyen}592]$ --- the \emph{whole range} outside the support.
\end{frame}

% [RELEASE R5 — the override reveal]
\begin{frame}{One More Thing, Says Wen}

Property revenue managers \textbf{override} the system on about \textbf{8\% of nights}.

\bigskip

They override on local knowledge: a wedding block, a competitor closing a wing, a conference that never reached the event calendar.

\bigskip

\begin{center}
The override is logged. \textcolor{warnred}{\textbf{The reason is not.}}
\end{center}

\bigskip

Return to the assumption from earlier:

\begin{center}
\emph{``The RM system is the only thing setting rates.''} \quad \textcolor{warnred}{\textbf{False.}}
\end{center}

\bigskip

Something moves both the rate and bookings, and it is not in $X$. No amount of flexibility in $g$ repairs that.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Key Takeaways}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \spinestep{Estimand.} A local elasticity, holding the system's signals fixed, held against the break-even. With heterogeneous slopes, a variance-weighted average over the nights where rates moved.
    \item \spinestep{Identification.} The RM system is the assignment mechanism. Unconfoundedness given its signals --- measured as of pricing time --- and overlap: rates must move off the rule.
    \item \spinestep{Estimation.} Controls must enter as flexibly as the system that set the treatment; residualise \emph{both} sides (the bias is second-order in the nuisance errors); \textbf{cross-fit}, with folds and SEs by night.
    \item \textbf{A good prediction is not a causal estimate.} Attempt 2 had the best $R^2$ and the wrong answer.
    \item \textbf{None of this survives an unrecorded confounder.} Sensitivity prices the doubt; it does not remove it.
\end{itemize}

\bigskip

\textbf{Next time:} rates the hotel did \emph{not} choose --- randomised variation, and what it can and cannot identify.
\end{frame}
