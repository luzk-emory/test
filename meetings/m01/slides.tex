% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The Decision}

A firm can send a coupon.
\begin{itemize}
  \item It costs $c$ each time it is redeemed.
  \item Each purchase earns margin $m$.
\end{itemize}

\bigskip

The number that settles the decision is the coupon's \textbf{effect on purchase probability}, held against a \textbf{break-even} that depends on who would have bought anyway.

\bigskip

Today:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item what that effect is, and for whom (the estimand);
  \item why comparing buyers who got the coupon with those who did not gets it wrong, and why a coin gets it right;
  \item causal graphs, for data where nobody tossed a coin.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Two Potential Outcomes per Unit}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
$i = 1, \dots, n$ & units (customers, members) \\[0.3em]
$D_i \in \{0, 1\}$ & treatment: 1 if treated, 0 if not \\[0.3em]
$Y_i(1)$ & potential outcome if treated \\[0.3em]
$Y_i(0)$ & potential outcome if not treated \\[0.3em]
$\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)$ & individual effect
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\bigskip

Both potential outcomes exist \textbf{before} treatment is assigned. Assignment only decides which one we see.

\bigskip

A causal effect compares the same unit in two states of the world, not two different units.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Name the Estimand Before the Data}

\begin{definition*}{1.1}{Estimand}
An estimand is a summary of potential outcomes for a named population. It specifies the units, the intervention and its comparator, the outcome and its horizon. It is fixed before, and independently of, any data or method.
\end{definition*}

\bigskip

Said in one sentence, for the worked example later today:

\medskip

\begin{quote}
The average effect of \textcolor{noteblue}{offering a free personal-training session}, against \textcolor{noteblue}{no offer}, on \textcolor{noteblue}{renewal at contract end}, among \textcolor{noteblue}{FitLife members whose contracts are about to expire}.
\end{quote}

\medskip

If you cannot write the sentence, no method will tell you what its output means.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Three Averages, Three Decisions}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Estimand} & \textbf{Definition} & \textbf{The decision it serves} \\
\midrule
$\ATE$ & $\E[Y(1) - Y(0)]$ & treat everyone? \\
$\ATT$ & $\E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid D = 1]$ & keep treating the people we treat now? \\
$\ATU$ & $\E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid D = 0]$ & extend treatment to the rest? \\
$\tau(x)$ & $\E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X = x]$ & whom to treat? (Meeting 3) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The ATT and ATU depend on the assignment mechanism, which defines who the treated are. The ATE does not.

\medskip

\begin{result}{1.2}{The ATE averages the ATT and ATU}
With $\pi = \Pr(D=1)$, \quad $\ATE = \pi\,\ATT + (1-\pi)\,\ATU$.
\end{result}

\small The population is the treated plus the untreated, so its average effect is the weighted average of the two groups' effects.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Which Population?}

\textbf{Finite population (design-based).} $\E$ averages over the $n$ units in the study. The estimand is the \emph{sample average treatment effect}
\[
\tau_S = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n \tau_i ,
\]
a fixed number: the answer about the customers in front of us. It needs no assumption about how they were sampled. Meetings 1 and 2 use this reading for experiments.

\bigskip

\textbf{Superpopulation.} The units are a sample from a larger population, and the estimand is that population's average.

\bigskip

The two coincide when the study is a random sample of the population the decision covers. Otherwise, moving to another population is a separate, assumed step called \textbf{transport} (Meeting 2).
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Four Response Types}

With a binary outcome, each unit has one of four pairs $(Y(0), Y(1))$.

\bigskip

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|c|c|}
\multicolumn{1}{c}{} & \multicolumn{1}{c}{$Y(1) = 0$} & \multicolumn{1}{c}{$Y(1) = 1$} \\
\cline{2-3}
$Y(0) = 0$ & \cellcolor{gray!15} Lost cause & \cellcolor{green!15} \textbf{Persuadable} \\
 & $\tau_i = 0$ & $\tau_i = +1$ \\
\cline{2-3}
$Y(0) = 1$ & \cellcolor{red!15} Do-not-disturb & \cellcolor{blue!10} Sure thing \\
 & $\tau_i = -1$ & $\tau_i = 0$ \\
\cline{2-3}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\bigskip

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Persuadable:} the coupon causes a purchase.
  \item \textbf{Sure thing:} buys either way; the coupon discounts a purchase that was coming.
  \item \textbf{Lost cause:} never buys; the coupon goes unused.
  \item \textbf{Do-not-disturb:} the coupon prevents a purchase.
\end{itemize}

\small A unit's type needs both potential outcomes, so it is never observed.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The ATE in Response Types}

\begin{result}{1.3}{The ATE in response types}
$\ATE = \Pr(\text{persuadable}) - \Pr(\text{do-not-disturb})$.
\end{result}

Only two types have a nonzero effect.

\bigskip

A lift of $0.10$ fits 10\% persuadables and no do-not-disturbs. It also fits 30\% and 20\%. Data identify the difference, not the two shares (bounds are in the notes, Section 3).

\bigskip

\textbf{Predicting who buys finds the wrong people.} A model of $\Pr(\text{purchase} \mid X)$ ranks sure things first: they have the highest purchase rate and zero effect. The decision needs the effect, the column the data never show.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{What a Treatment Is Worth}

Margin $m$ per purchase; coupon cost $c$ per redemption, paid only when a treated unit buys; $p_1 = \E[Y(1)]$ and $p_0 = \E[Y(0)]$ for the population to be treated.

\medskip

\begin{result}{1.4}{Net value and break-even}
The expected net value per unit treated, relative to not treating, is
\[
  V = m\tau - c\,p_1 = (m-c)\,\tau - c\,p_0 ,
\]
and treating pays if and only if $\tau > \tau^\ast = c\,p_1/m$.
\end{result}

\medskip

The second form separates what the coupon does:
\begin{itemize}
  \item it earns $m-c$ on every purchase it \emph{causes};
  \item it loses $c$ on every purchase that would have happened anyway: the \textbf{sure-thing cost} $c\,p_0$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Who Costs What}

Change in margin from treating one unit, with $m = 30$ and $c = 10$ paid per redemption:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Type} & \textbf{Untreated} & \textbf{Treated} & \textbf{Change} \\
\midrule
Persuadable & 0 & $m-c = 20$ & \textcolor{sbgreen}{$+20$} \\
Sure thing & $m = 30$ & $m-c = 20$ & \textcolor{warnred}{$-10$} \\
Lost cause & 0 & 0 & $0$ \\
Do-not-disturb & $m = 30$ & 0 & \textcolor{warnred}{$-30$} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

A lost cause costs nothing when the treatment is paid only on use. If it costs $c$ per unit \emph{treated} (a phone call, a trainer's hour), then $V = m\tau - c$ and $\tau^\ast = c/m$.

\medskip

\begin{itemize}
  \item Always state which cost model applies.
  \item In both, the break-even depends on the population. Hold a lift against the break-even of the population the decision covers.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{One Potential Outcome Is Always Missing}

We observe
\[
Y_i = D_i\, Y_i(1) + (1 - D_i)\, Y_i(0) .
\]
Causal inference is a missing-data problem in which the missing values are missing \emph{by design}.

\medskip

\textbf{At the board.} Four records. Fill in what was observed, then the types each record allows.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{crrccl}
\toprule
\textbf{Unit} & $D$ & $Y$ & $Y(1)$ & $Y(0)$ & \textbf{Compatible types} \\
\midrule
1 & 1 & 1 & 1 & ? & sure thing or persuadable \\
2 & 0 & 1 & ? & 1 & sure thing or do-not-disturb \\
3 & 1 & 0 & 0 & ? & lost cause or do-not-disturb \\
4 & 0 & 0 & ? & 0 & lost cause or persuadable \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

More units help estimate \textbf{averages}. They never reveal unit 1's missing outcome.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{What Writing $Y_i(d)$ Assumes}

\begin{assumption}{1.5}{SUTVA}
(i) \emph{No interference}: unit $i$'s outcome depends only on its own treatment. (ii) \emph{Consistency}: ``treated'' names one well-defined intervention, so $Y_i = Y_i(d)$ when $D_i = d$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{No interference fails} when a coupon is shared with friends, or a discount at one store draws shoppers from the next (Handout H4).
  \item \textbf{Consistency fails} when ``a coupon'' is sometimes 10\% off and sometimes 30\%. The effect is then a mixture over versions, weighted by whatever produced the data.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

\textbf{Working rule:} describe the intervention precisely enough that you could, in principle, assign it.

\medskip

\small Attributes such as race or loyalty are not interventions. An audit study randomises a \emph{signal} of the attribute instead, such as a name on comparable r\'esum\'es, and estimates the effect of the signal on a reader's decision (notes, Section 3).
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The Naive Comparison}

\begin{result}{1.6}{Selection-bias decomposition}
Under consistency,
\[
  \E[Y \mid D=1] - \E[Y \mid D=0] = \underbrace{\ATT}_{\text{effect on the treated}}
  + \underbrace{\E[Y(0) \mid D=1] - \E[Y(0) \mid D=0]}_{\text{selection bias}} .
\]
\end{result}

The idea: add and subtract $\E[Y(0) \mid D=1]$, the one quantity the data never show.

\bigskip

Selection bias asks how the treated and untreated would have differed \emph{without} treatment.
\begin{itemize}
  \item A manager who treats units that would do well anyway makes it positive.
  \item One who treats struggling units makes it negative, and can reverse the sign.
  \item It does not shrink as the sample grows.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Two Sources of Bias Relative to the ATE}

\begin{result}{1.7}{Two sources of bias relative to the ATE}
Under consistency,
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \E[Y \mid D=1] - \E[Y \mid D=0] - \ATE &= \underbrace{\E[Y(0) \mid D=1] - \E[Y(0) \mid D=0]}_{\text{baseline selection}}\\
  &\quad + \underbrace{(1-\pi)(\ATT - \ATU)}_{\text{selection on gains}} .
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{result}

\bigskip

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Baseline selection:} the treated would have had different outcomes anyway.
  \item \textbf{Selection on gains:} the treated respond more (or less) than the untreated would.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

The first term is Result 1.6. The second comes from $\ATT - \ATE = (1-\pi)(\ATT - \ATU)$, which is Result 1.2 rearranged.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{A Coin Removes Both}

\begin{assumption}{1.8}{Random assignment}
$D \indep \{Y(0), Y(1)\}$ and $0 < \Pr(D=1) < 1$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{result}{1.9}{Identification by randomisation}
Under SUTVA and random assignment, $\ATE = \E[Y \mid D=1] - \E[Y \mid D=0]$, and $\ATE = \ATT = \ATU$.
\end{result}

\bigskip

By consistency, $\E[Y \mid D=d] = \E[Y(d) \mid D=d]$. Independence then replaces this with $\E[Y(d)]$, because who got treated carries no information about potential outcomes. Both bias terms in Result 1.7 are zero.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Why a Coin Can Be Trusted}

Independence is a property of the \textbf{procedure}, not of the customers. A random number generator does not know anyone's potential outcomes.

\bigskip

\begin{itemize}
  \item That is why randomisation removes confounding by variables \emph{nobody recorded}.
  \item It can be audited rather than argued for: the seed, the draw log, no exclusions after the draw.
  \item Independence holds over the distribution of possible draws. Any single draw can still be unbalanced by chance, and standard errors measure how much that matters (Meeting 2).
\end{itemize}

\bigskip

Every other design in this course replaces the coin with an assumption about the world. Those assumptions have to be argued, because they cannot be audited.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{FitLife: The Estimands and the Naive Comparison}

\step{Estimands.} $\ATE = 3/8 = 0.375$; \quad $\ATT = 1/4 = 0.25$; \quad $\ATU = 2/4 = 0.50$.

\smallskip
\small Check Result 1.2: $0.5 \times 0.25 + 0.5 \times 0.50 = 0.375$.
\normalsize

\bigskip

\step{Naive comparison.} Treated renewal rate $4/4 = 1.00$; untreated $1/4 = 0.25$; difference $0.75$.

\bigskip

\begin{align*}
\text{Result 1.6:}\quad 0.75 &= \underbrace{0.25}_{\ATT} + \underbrace{3/4 - 1/4}_{\text{selection bias} \,=\, 0.50} \\[0.3em]
\text{Result 1.7:}\quad 0.75 &= \underbrace{0.375}_{\ATE} + \underbrace{0.50 + 0.5 \times (0.25 - 0.50)}_{\text{bias relative to the ATE} \,=\, 0.375}
\end{align*}

\bigskip

Selection bias is $0.50$: three of the four members offered a session would have renewed without it, against one of the four who were not.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{FitLife: The Decision}

Taken at face value, the naive $0.75$ makes the programme look like a clear success. Against the break-even of $0.30$:

\bigskip

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcl}
\toprule
\textbf{Who gets the session} & \textbf{Effect} & \textbf{Against $\tau^\ast = 0.30$} \\
\midrule
The members staff choose ($\ATT$) & $0.25$ & \textcolor{warnred}{loses money} \\
The members staff ignore ($\ATU$) & $0.50$ & \textcolor{sbgreen}{pays} \\
Everyone ($\ATE$) & $0.375$ & \textcolor{sbgreen}{pays} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\bigskip

\pause

The programme as run loses money. The selection that inflated the naive number also put the sessions in the wrong hands.

\medskip

A randomised offer would have shown this.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{End of Block A}

\begin{itemize}
  \item An estimand names units, intervention, comparator, outcome and horizon, before any data.
  \item Value depends on the cost model: per redemption, $\tau^\ast = c\,p_1/m$; per unit treated, $\tau^\ast = c/m$.
  \item Naive comparison $=$ ATT $+$ selection bias. More data does not shrink the bias.
  \item A coin sets both bias terms to zero, and its log can be audited.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{A Graph Writes Down Assumptions}

A \textbf{directed acyclic graph} (DAG) states what causes what.

\bigskip

\begin{itemize}
  \item An arrow $A \to B$ says $A$ may directly cause $B$.
  \item A \emph{missing} arrow says it does not. Missing arrows are the strong claims.
  \item Acyclic: no variable causes itself through a loop.
\end{itemize}

\bigskip

The graph is a claim about the world, not something estimated from the data. Two analysts with different graphs can reach different conclusions from the same table, and the graph shows exactly where they disagree.

\bigskip

Three building blocks decide whether two variables are associated.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Chain, Fork, Collider}

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[>={Stealth[length=2mm]}, every node/.style={font=\small}]
  \node (a1) at (0,0) {$A$}; \node (m1) at (1.3,0) {$M$}; \node (b1) at (2.6,0) {$B$};
  \draw[->] (a1) -- (m1); \draw[->] (m1) -- (b1); \node at (1.3,-1.0) {\textbf{Chain}};
  \node (a2) at (4.6,0) {$A$}; \node (f2) at (5.9,0.8) {$F$}; \node (b2) at (7.2,0) {$B$};
  \draw[->] (f2) -- (a2); \draw[->] (f2) -- (b2); \node at (5.9,-1.0) {\textbf{Fork}};
  \node (a3) at (9.2,0) {$A$}; \node (c3) at (10.5,-0.6) {$C$}; \node (b3) at (11.8,0) {$B$};
  \draw[->] (a3) -- (c3); \draw[->] (b3) -- (c3); \node at (10.5,-1.2) {\textbf{Collider}};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\medskip

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{llcl}
\toprule
\textbf{Structure} & \textbf{Middle node} & \textbf{$A$, $B$ associated?} & \textbf{Condition on the middle node} \\
\midrule
Chain $A \to M \to B$ & mediator & yes & blocks the association \\
Fork $A \leftarrow F \to B$ & common cause & yes & blocks it \\
Collider $A \to C \leftarrow B$ & common effect & \textbf{no} & \textcolor{warnred}{\textbf{creates} an association} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

In a fork, $A$ and $B$ are associated although neither causes the other. For a collider, conditioning on anything $C$ causes has the same effect as conditioning on $C$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{When Is a Path Open?}

A \textbf{path} is any sequence of arrows between two variables, whatever their direction.

\bigskip

A path is \textbf{blocked} by
\begin{itemize}
  \item a middle node of a chain or fork that we condition on, or
  \item a collider that we do \emph{not} condition on (nor on any of its descendants).
\end{itemize}

\bigskip

Two variables are associated, given a conditioning set, only through unblocked paths.

\bigskip

\small Conditioning means comparing within levels of a variable: a control in a regression, a filter on the data, a sample restricted to one group. All three have the same consequences on the graph.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Collider Bias in the Simplest Case}

\begin{result}{1.10}{Collider bias in the simplest case}
Let $D$ and $U$ be independent with $\Pr(D=1) = \Pr(U=1) = \tfrac12$, let $Y = U$ (so $D$ has no effect), and let $C = \max(D, U)$. Then
\[
\E[Y \mid D=1, C=1] - \E[Y \mid D=0, C=1] = \tfrac12 - 1 = -\tfrac12 .
\]
\end{result}

\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{cccc}
\toprule
$D$ & $U = Y$ & $C$ & kept if $C=1$ \\
\midrule
0 & 0 & 0 & no \\
0 & 1 & 1 & yes \\
1 & 0 & 1 & yes \\
1 & 1 & 1 & yes \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.54\textwidth}
Four equally likely cells. Among units with $C = 1$, learning $D = 0$ tells you $U = 1$.

\medskip

$D$ has no effect on $Y$, yet the comparison within $C = 1$ finds one.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backdoor Paths}

A \textbf{backdoor path} from $D$ to $Y$ is a path that starts with an arrow \emph{into} $D$.

\bigskip

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[>={Stealth[length=2mm]}, every node/.style={font=\small}]
  \node (x) at (0,1.3) {$X$};
  \node (d) at (-2.2,0) {$D$};
  \node (y) at (2.2,0) {$Y$};
  \node (m) at (0,-1.1) {$M$};
  \draw[->] (x) -- (d); \draw[->] (x) -- (y);
  \draw[->, thick, sbgreen] (d) -- (y);
  \draw[->, thick, sbgreen] (d) -- (m); \draw[->, thick, sbgreen] (m) -- (y);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
  \item $D \to Y$ and $D \to M \to Y$ carry the effect of $D$. Leave them open.
  \item $D \leftarrow X \to Y$ is a backdoor path: an association that is not an effect. Block it by conditioning on $X$.
  \item Conditioning on $M$ would block part of the effect: $M$ is caused by $D$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Adjustment for a Backdoor Set}

\begin{result}{1.11}{Adjustment for a backdoor set}
Suppose a set $X$ of variables (i) contains nothing caused by $D$ and (ii) blocks every backdoor path from $D$ to $Y$. Then conditional exchangeability holds, $\{Y(0), Y(1)\} \indep D \mid X$, and if $0 < \Pr(D=1 \mid X) < 1$,
\[
  \E[Y(d)] = \sum_x \E[Y \mid D=d, X=x]\,\Pr(X=x) .
\]
\end{result}

\bigskip

\begin{itemize}
  \item Condition (ii) closes the non-causal paths. Condition (i) keeps the causal paths open and avoids opening a collider.
  \item Within a level of $X$, treated and untreated units are comparable, as if a coin had been tossed within each level.
  \item $0 < \Pr(D=1 \mid X) < 1$: every level of $X$ needs both treated and untreated units.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

The formula in Result 1.11 is \textbf{standardisation}: compare within levels of $X$, then average with the population's weights. Using the treated units' weights instead gives the ATT.

\begin{frame}{Simpson's Paradox}

An association can \textbf{reverse} when the data are split by a third variable. Which answer is right is decided by the graph, not by the data.

\bigskip

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{The third variable is} & \textbf{Graph} & \textbf{Right comparison} \\
\midrule

a mediator & $D \to M \to Y$ & pooled; splitting is wrong \\
a collider & $D \to C \leftarrow Y$ & pooled; splitting is wrong \\
\bottomrule

\end{center}

The same table of numbers can come from any of the three graphs. Knowing which one you are in is subject-matter knowledge.

\begin{frame}{Estimation: What the Randomisation Guarantees}

In a \textbf{completely randomised experiment} exactly $n_1$ of $n$ units are treated, each subset equally likely. Hold the potential outcomes fixed: the only randomness is the draw.

\medskip

\begin{result}{1.12}{Unbiasedness over the design}
$\hatt = \bar Y_1 - \bar Y_0$ satisfies $\E_D[\hatt] = \tau_S$, where $\E_D$ averages over draws.
\end{result}

\medskip

\begin{itemize}
  \item The argument uses nothing but the draw: no outcome model, no sampling assumption, no large-sample approximation.
  \item The collection of $\hatt$ over all possible draws is the \textbf{randomisation distribution}. Its centre is $\tau_S$; its spread is what a standard error estimates (Meeting 2).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The Regression Computes the Same Number}

\begin{result}{1.13}{OLS on a dummy is the difference in means}
In the least-squares fit of $Y_i = \alpha + \beta D_i + \varepsilon_i$, \quad $\hat\alpha = \bar Y_0$ and $\hat\beta = \bar Y_1 - \bar Y_0$.
\end{result}

\bigskip

The regression is a computing device. Whether its coefficient is causal is decided by identification, not by the software.

\bigskip

The omitted-variable-bias formula is Result 1.6 written for a regression. For binary $D$,
\[
\frac{\Cov(D,Y)}{\Var(D)} = \E[Y(0) \mid D=1] - \E[Y(0) \mid D=0] + \ATT .
\]
On FitLife's session data the regression returns $0.75$, the naive comparison, with the same $0.50$ of selection bias inside it.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Identification, Estimation, Inference}

Keep the three questions apart.

\bigskip

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{p{2.8cm}p{9.8cm}}
\toprule

\step{Estimation} & How is the comparison computed from finite data? \\[0.5em]
\step{Inference} & How far is the estimate likely to be from the truth because of chance? \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\bigskip

A sophisticated estimator cannot rescue a failed identification argument, and a small standard error does not make a biased comparison right.

\begin{frame}{Three Common Errors}

\begin{pitfall}{three common errors}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Holding a lift against zero instead of the break-even of the population the decision covers.
  \item ``Controlling for everything available'': a mediator removes part of the effect and a collider adds bias.
  \item Reading a large sample as protection against selection bias. It only makes the wrong number more precise.
\end{itemize}
\end{pitfall}

\bigskip

Each one has appeared today: the break-even of $0.30$ at FitLife, the class-attendance proposal, and the naive $0.75$, which a larger sample would only make more precise.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \step{Estimand.} Name the number the decision needs, for whom, before any data. The break-even depends on the population and the cost model.
  \item \step{Identification.} Naive comparison $=$ ATT $+$ selection bias. A coin removes the bias by procedure. Without one, a graph says what to compare within: block backdoor paths; never condition on a mediator or a collider.
  \item \step{Estimation.} The difference in means equals OLS on a dummy and is unbiased over draws of the coin. A single draw can miss.
  \item \textbf{An effect can be real and unprofitable.} At FitLife the sessions worked and still lost money: staff gave them to members who would have renewed anyway.
\end{itemize}
