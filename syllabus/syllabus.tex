\section*{What this course is about}

This is a course in causal inference for business in the age of AI. AI now makes prediction and
code cheap. What remains scarce is knowing what a number means for a decision: which comparison
identifies the effect you need, and whether the analysis that produced it can be trusted. The
course therefore trains two skills above all others: \emph{identification} (where does the
counterfactual come from, and what must be true for it to be right?) and \emph{verification} (how
do you check an estimate, a report or a piece of code, including code you did not write?).

Earlier courses taught models that predict. This course asks what will happen \emph{if we act}:
whether a coupon causes purchases, which customers to target and at what cost, whether a price
change pays, whether an AI tool was worth rolling out. Each of these needs a counterfactual: what
would have happened without the action. The course is organised around where that counterfactual
comes from. Experimental identification comes first and observational methods follow.

\textbf{Lecture cases and worked examples.} The lectures follow running cases: a coffee chain
deciding who gets a coupon (Meetings 1--7), a hotel group setting its rates (Meeting 8
\tbd{and Meeting 9}), and a home-goods retail chain rolling out an AI assistant (Meetings 10--11).
The technical notes work their examples in different businesses, a fitness-club chain and a
food-delivery platform, so that you see each method carry over to a new setting. Every meeting opens
with a decision and the number that settles it, and every method arrives because a simpler one
failed to deliver that number.

\textbf{Generative AI.} Generative AI enters in two distinct ways, and the course keeps them apart.
A generative model trained on real logs can act as a \emph{simulator}, with the effect and the
confounding planted by the analyst: a testbed for an estimator, or for a whole
predict-then-optimise pipeline. Asking a language model for counterfactuals is a different thing,
because there the ``truth'' is the model's prior rather than something planted. Language models
also generate \emph{variables}: labels and embeddings that are measurements with error, whose
consequences depend on the variable's role. Meeting 9 covers both. Lectures stay focused on causal
reasoning; hands-on work with language models and agents happens in the labs, the assignments and
the final project, where AI appears in five roles: as a \emph{tool} (Lab 1), a
\emph{decision-maker} (Lab 4), an \emph{analyst} whose work you audit (Lab 6), a \emph{source of
data} (Lab 8) and an \emph{intervention} to evaluate (Lab 9).

\section*{Learning objectives}

By the end of the course you should be able to:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item state the counterfactual a business decision needs, and the break-even that decides it;
    \item estimate average and conditional treatment effects from experiments, with honest uncertainty;
    \item turn effect estimates into a targeting policy under costs, caps, budgets and capacity, and
    evaluate that policy, including one chosen by an automated system, on data it was not chosen on;
    \item estimate effects from observational data by adjustment, propensity scores, double machine
    learning, difference-in-differences and synthetic control;
    \item tie each method's identifying assumption to specific facts about how the data were
    generated, and say how wrong it would have to be to change the decision;
    \item read a report that makes a causal claim, identify the comparison it rests on, and name
    what else could produce the pattern, with the mechanism and the direction of the bias;
    \item verify an analysis you did not write by hand, using tests against a planted effect; use a
    learned simulator as a testbed; and correct an estimate that uses a model-coded variable;
    \item write a recommendation that says what was estimated, for whom, and what could overturn it.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Prerequisites.} \tbd{Part I of Applied ML for Business, or equivalent: regression,
cross-validation, tree ensembles, and Python.} Lab 0 reviews the Python, probability and
statistics the course uses.

\section*{How a meeting runs}

Each meeting has three blocks: A (minutes 0--50), B (60--110) and C (120--180), with ten-minute
breaks between them. Blocks A and B are lectures. Block C is a lab (Labs 0--10; see the table below).
\begin{itemize}
    \item In Meetings 5 and 11, block A is an exam.
    \item In Meeting 8, block C is the project proposal presentations; Lab 7 is done as a take-home.
    \item Meeting 12 is the final presentations.
\end{itemize}

Case material is released in stages during class. Worksheets ask you to attempt a calculation before
the slides explain it: commit to your answer in writing, even when you are unsure.

\textbf{Reading and critique.} Some meetings open with a short reading-and-critique exercise, about
15 minutes, done on paper without AI or devices. \tbd{Which meetings: to be set (blocks A of
Meetings 5 and 11 are exams).} You read a one- or two-page case, write individual notes, discuss in
pairs, then as a class. The questions are always the same:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item What is the claim?
    \item What causal question does the decision need?
    \item Who is the comparison group?
    \item What else could produce this pattern? Give the mechanism and the direction of the bias.
    \item What would you do to find out?
\end{enumerate}
Early cases contain one flaw, matched to a concept already taught. Later cases are excerpts of real
reports with several flaws, which you rank by how much they matter for the decision. Occasionally a
report is sound, and saying so, with reasons, is the right answer.

\textbf{Technical notes and handouts.} Each meeting has a set of technical notes, released after the
meeting. Section~1 states the general results, with short proofs of the key ones, and is
examinable. Section~2 is a worked example in a different business, with exercises, and is
examinable. Section~3 holds extensions and annotated further reading and is not examinable. A
one-page notation sheet is shared by all notes. Six handouts treat topics that stand on their own;
they are for projects and further study and are \emph{not} examined:

\subsection*{Assignments}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em, style=nextline]
\item[A1: Write once, then delegate] Out after Meeting 2; due Friday of Week 2. Covers Meetings
1--2. On a second email campaign, hand-code the ATE with a robust standard error, and CUPED. Then
have a language model generate the full readout (sample-ratio check, ATE, CUPED, test against the
break-even, minimum detectable effect for a follow-up) and verify it with a simulation test suite.
One-page decision memo.
\item[A2: Coupon whom, and at what budget?] Out after Meeting 5; due Sunday of Week 4. Covers
Meetings 3--6. Conditional effects from two meta-learners and a causal forest; the ranking evaluated
with Qini curves and RATE; a budgeted allocation and a depth-2 policy tree. You submit frozen
policies, which an autograder scores on a locked randomised evaluation set. Memo on value, cost, and
what could overturn the recommendation.
\item[A3: Learn the policy from the logs] Out after Meeting 8; due Sunday of Week 5. Covers
Meetings 6--8. The same decision from observational logs only: a DAG and an adjustment set; average
and conditional effects by regression, AIPW and DML; overlap and trimming. Your policy is scored on
the same locked set, next to a policy trained on the randomised data. Sensitivity: how strong hidden
confounding must be to change the chosen policy.
\item[A4: Stress-test the pricing pipeline] Out after Meeting 9; due Sunday of Week 6. Covers
Meetings 8--9. Two pretrained hotel-demand simulators (a neural and a boosted outcome model), each
with a dial for hidden confounding. Run predict-then-optimise, DML-then-optimise and one pipeline of
your choice; measure revenue regret against the oracle across confounding levels; check whether the
ranking of pipelines holds across simulators and outside the logged price range. Recommend a
pipeline and a deployment check (a holdout or a switchback).
\end{description}

\subsection*{Final project}

You work in pairs on a causal question with business stakes, using a design from the course, and
write an industry-style report. You may use AI however you choose, with disclosure (see the AI use
policy). Designs from the handouts (instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, staggered
adoption) are acceptable, supported by the handout and instructor feedback.

The report has two required appendices:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item \textbf{Review record.} A language model's review of your draft, with your response to each
    point (accepted and fixed, or rejected with reasons).
    \item \textbf{Planted-effect validation.} Your design run on data with an effect you planted,
    showing that it recovers the effect, or how far it misses.
\end{enumerate}
Agreement with a tool earns no credit by itself; the quality of your reasoning does.
